Machine learning · University of Turku

Blood pressure estimation from PPG signals

Estimated systolic and diastolic blood pressure from photoplethysmogram (PPG) recordings, the optical pulse signal that smartwatches and pulse oximeters measure. This was a pair project: the blood pressure estimation was my part, and my partner worked on a separate analysis. The picture shows one 10-second recording before and after filtering.

  • Data: 29,599 PPG segments of 10 seconds at 125 Hz, split into 23,675 for training and 5,924 for testing.
  • Preprocessing in Python: a Butterworth band-pass filter, wavelet denoising and Gaussian smoothing.
  • 23 engineered features per segment: pulse shape (systolic and diastolic amplitude, augmentation index, rise and fall time, dicrotic notch), statistics (skewness, kurtosis, entropy), frequency features and signal derivatives.
  • Models: support vector regression and gradient boosting. The best mean absolute error was 19.2 mmHg for systolic and 7.5 mmHg for diastolic pressure.
  • Honest evaluation: the predictions stayed close to the average, with a concordance index of about 0.5, so the models had not learned the variation between recordings. I traced the likely causes to the features, model complexity and signal quality, and tested other feature sets, models and preprocessing.

Web app · JavaScript

Chess Game Analyser

Paste a game and Stockfish 19, compiled to WebAssembly and running in the browser, evaluates every position. Each move gets a classification from Best to Blunder, and each player gets an accuracy score computed with the same win-probability model Lichess uses.

  • A coach that sorts your mistakes by why they happened: hanging pieces, missed threats, missed tactics. It turns the costliest patterns into questions to ask at the board.
  • Reads clock data to find habits such as blundering in instant moves or in time trouble.
  • Saved games are grouped by opening, with score and accuracy per opening and per game phase.
  • Interactive evaluation graph, best-move arrows, keyboard navigation, light and dark mode, and unit tests.

Bachelor's thesis · Åbo Akademi · 2024

Using OpenPose for sprint running analysis

Can a computer vision model measure sprint technique from ordinary video? In this literature-based thesis I examined OpenPose, an open-source deep learning method that detects body keypoints in images and video, and asked whether it can measure the joint angles that matter in sprinting.

  • Covered sprint biomechanics, including hip, knee and ankle angles in the block start, acceleration and maximum-velocity phases.
  • Explained the deep learning underneath: neural networks, convolutional networks, and OpenPose's architecture with confidence maps and part affinity fields.
  • Reviewed three studies of OpenPose on running: sprints filmed with a smartphone, hurdling across network backbones (VGG19 and MobileNet, compared by RMSE), and treadmill running compared with marker-based motion capture.
  • Conclusion: OpenPose is not yet accurate enough for sprint analysis. Accuracy drops as speed rises, even from 2.78 to 3.33 m/s, and missing values and false positives make joint angles unreliable.
